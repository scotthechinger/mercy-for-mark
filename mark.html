Mark Allen Jenkins

Mark is fifty-eight years old and he has been on death row since he was twenty-three.

This is his story: who he is today, what was done to him first, what he did, and what he has made of thirty-five years incarcerated.

Explore his story↓

Parts of this page may be difficult to read. It describes the abuse Mark suffered as a child, including sexual abuse, beatings and violence inside his family, and it discusses the crime for which Mark was convicted.

01.

Today: Who Mark is now

Mark Jenkins is fifty-eight. He is incarcerated at Holman Correctional Facility in Atmore, Alabama and has been since 1991. For much of that time, he has worked as a “hall runner,” a job given only to the men the officers trust, the ones who stay out of trouble and can be let out of a cell to move around the tier.

Mark defuses arguments, lifts people up when they are down, tells corny jokes, likes NASCAR and the San Francisco 49ers, and has been, in the words of one officer, “100% trustworthy.” He was baptized at his own request in the county jail before his trial and has grown into the Christian man he is today.

Mark has learned to contend with his profound intellectual disability and the abuse and trauma of his childhood to continue to serve as a support and helper to the officers and other incarcerated people around him.

02.

Before: A childhood of extreme abuse and violence

Mark Jenkins was born in 1967, the product of an affair between his mother, Donna Jo Jenkins, and a sailor. At the time, Donna's husband, Steve Jenkins, was in prison for armed robbery. Mark has never met his biological father.

Following his birth, Donna abandoned Mark at the hospital. She was later persuaded to retrieve him, but his biological father declined to take responsibility for the newborn. Left alone, Donna raised Mark and his half-siblings in squalor for the first three years of his life, until her husband returned from prison.

Donna treated Mark with contempt and abused him, even when he was an infant. She regularly beat him, at times hard enough to leave welts on his body. She shook him, called him disparaging names, and hid him in a box under the bathroom sink whenever company came to visit. She refused to bathe him. She treated him as though he were barely human.

When Mark was about four years old, he was left in the care of his step-grandfather, who sexually abused him. The sexual trauma, and the fact that nobody helped him afterward, led to severe emotional and physical struggles that lasted into adulthood.

During Mark's trial, his attorneys did not tell the jury a single word about it, or about anything that might have led to the moment in which he took an innocent life.

02.

Before: A childhood of extreme abuse and violence

The physical and emotional torment intensified after his father's release from prison. He beat and humiliated Mark on an almost daily basis, regularly leaving welts, bruises and cuts. Mark was not allowed to eat with the family; he scrounged for scraps or ate dog food.

At least once, Steve forced Mark to eat his own feces in front of the family and extended relatives.

Family members reported the abuse to child protective services, only to recant when threatened.

When Mark's older brother reported it, their father threatened to kill the children, saying, “I'll kill you before I go back to prison.”

He did have one saving grace as a child: his animals. He had a soul-deep love of the family's horses, dogs, and a pig named Eva, and the animals loved him back. Even when he had barely any food, he shared what he had with the family dogs.

Mark's struggles were compounded by his profound intellectual disability, identified as early as eight in school records but never adequately addressed. His IQ is just 69.8, and his executive functioning, which includes planning and reasoning, is at or below the 1st percentile. The record of disability

His disability is not a claim made for this case

It was measured and written down long before any of this mattered to a court, by the schools that held him back and by the neuropsychologist who examined him over two days at Holman. Select any passage to read it.

Forensic neuropsychological assessment of Mark Jenkins, Robert H. Ouaou, Ph.D., May 2023.

Dr. Ouaou is a clinical and forensic neuropsychologist with more than twenty-five years of experience. He examined Mark at Holman over two days in November 2022 and reviewed the records of his childhood. His conclusion was that Mark has an intellectual disability, that it began before he was eighteen, and that it continues to this day. What that means for clemency.

03.

Mark's crime

Mark began running away around age eleven to escape the chaos of his parents' home.

At times he lived on the streets and begged for change.

When he turned eighteen he tried to turn his life around and enlist in the armed forces, but he failed the entrance exam. A little while later a friend asked him to help the family move to Alabama, and Mark went with them.

There he tried to build a new life, but he was taken advantage of by employers and started drinking heavily and regularly.

April 18, 1989

Mark had been drinking heavily and drove to a diner where Tammy Hogeland worked. He was twenty-one years old. She was twenty-two.

Tammy left the diner with him. At some point the two got into an argument. During that argument, Mark strangled Tammy to death.

Tammy Hogeland was a young woman with a life ahead of her, and it was taken from her. Nothing on this website is offered to diminish that, and nothing here asks anyone to forget it.

In 1991, Mark was convicted of capital murder in St. Clair County and sentenced to death. Mark was alone at his trial. Not one member of his family came.

Mark was horrified by his actions from the moment he became conscious of them and feels tremendous remorse to this day.

04.

Thirty-five years: Atonement

Those who know him, from family members to correctional officers, have seen how his remorse for the terrible harm he caused has stayed with him over the decades.

Since his incarceration, Mark has worked relentlessly to atone for his crime: building positive relationships, supporting the correctional officers and the men around him, and growing in his Christian faith.

He has become a different, and better, man than the one who arrived on death row more than thirty-five years ago.

That is not a claim his lawyers are making about him. It is the claim made, independently and in their own handwriting, by sixty people who were paid to watch him.
